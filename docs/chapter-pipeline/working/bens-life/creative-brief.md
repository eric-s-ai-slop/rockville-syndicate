# BEN’S LIFE — CREATIVE CHAPTER BRIEF

## Logline

The group looks back on the original Ben era—from pressure-cooker plans and parental internet countermeasures to sippy cups, Formula One delusions, and a car battery carried to school—and tries to determine how one person generated this much material.

## Structure

**Portrait anthology / chronological reminiscence**

The chapter is structured like a playable roast compilation rather than a single causal incident. Chronology provides forward movement, while comic escalation provides the spine: every era introduces a more advanced and less defensible version of Ben.

### Act I — Early Ben: Restricted Areas

**Time span:** Middle school through freshman year
**Primary locations:** 51 Monroe, school, group conversations

Ben becomes obsessed with reaching the roof of 51 Monroe. A locked door escalates into hypothetical explosives, weapons, and hostages. His barely functioning iPhone SE establishes that even Ben’s ordinary possessions become lore.

### Act II — Quarantine Ben: Counter-Surveillance

**Time span:** Quarantine
**Primary locations:** Ben’s bedroom, Discord, Rust

Ben’s parents restrict his internet. Ben responds with a Raspberry Pi, fake bedtime procedures, a locked bedroom door, and failed operational security.

The act peaks with:

> “MICHAEL IS PICKING MY LOCK.”

A second scene has Ben accidentally broadcasting a private conversation with Michael and Yvonne through his webcam.

### Act III — Ben Becomes Ben

**Time span:** Summer after sophomore year
**Primary locations:** Ocean City, school, Ben’s home

The recognizable Ben uniform appears: foul Brooks and wife beaters.

Ben drinks toilet water through a straw in front of the Ocean City girls and becomes seriously ill afterward. His Formula One fixation begins producing visible G-force training with resistance bands around his neck.

### Act IV — The Golden Age of Ben

**Time span:** Junior year
**Primary locations:** Snapchat, Mahargo’s house, Halloween party, school classroom, group chat

This is the chapter’s largest act and comic center.

Ben overanalyzes Colleen’s dry snaps, misses a pool ball “for Colleen,” is publicly identified as the kid nobody remembers inviting, gets drunk alone on “parents’ alc,” claims “I’m hip,” and is immediately denied social credibility.

The act continues through Maria Brooke, the brown grocery bag, the sippy cup, and the existing **Things Ben Can Do / Things Ben Can’t Do** minigame.

It culminates in Ben’s complete Formula One career plan:

* Approximately $15 per hour at a distant racing facility.
* More access to racing and experience.
* Sleeping beneath a bridge to solve housing.
* Saving for racing equipment, potentially without owning the vehicle.
* Eventual success in Dubai.
* Bitches on yachts.
* Dubai bitches.

### Act V — Senior Ben: Portable Electrical Hazard

**Time span:** Senior year
**Primary location:** Engineering or technology classroom

Ben carries a car battery to school inside his backpack.

Under the supervision of an indifferent long-term substitute, he shorts the terminals, creates sparks, and passes current through graphite until it becomes extremely bright and begins smoking.

This is the absurd chronological climax.

### Coda — The Overlook

**Time span:** Near the end of the original Ben era
**Primary location:** Scenic overlook off I-270 toward Frederick

Maharko recounts the only major Ben story the rest of the group did not witness.

Ben asks him to turn off the lights and says:

> “Dude, dude, just go to sleep.”

Ben says he cannot return home. Maharko feels unusually uncomfortable, remains there for a while, and eventually drives Ben home.

The scene is brief and observational. It does not explain Ben, transform the chapter into a tragedy, or retroactively justify his behavior.

Afterward, the narrator notes that Ben gradually drifted away. The original Ben era ends without a formal conclusion.

## Emotional Arc

**Start:** Excited recognition—“This was the first unmistakable Ben incident.”

**Escalation:** Each memory makes the previous memory appear reasonable.

**Comic peak:** The Formula One career plan and the whiteboard era establish that every part of Ben’s existence has entered group culture.

**Absurd climax:** Ben brings a car battery into school and creates smoking graphite beneath the supervision of an adult who does not care.

**Brief tonal pause:** The overlook story makes the roast hesitate for a moment.

**End:** Amused nostalgia for the quantity of material Ben produced, not emotional longing for Ben himself.

The intended balance is approximately:

* **90% roast**
* **8% nostalgia**
* **2% discomfort**

## Spine and Threads

### Primary spine — Ben Levels Up

The chapter tracks the evolution of Ben’s problem-solving:

1. A building door is locked.
2. His internet is restricted.
3. Professional racing is inaccessible.
4. Housing near a racetrack is unaffordable.
5. Electrical experimentation requires equipment.

Ben never abandons an objective because the plan is unreasonable. He introduces another unreasonable solution.

### Secondary spine — Everything Becomes Ben Lore

The group initially mocks isolated actions.

By junior year, Ben’s shoes, shirt, lunch bag, cup, flirting, employment plans, family alcohol, and individual sentences have all become reusable cultural artifacts.

The group no longer needs a major incident. Ben merely has to arrive holding something.

### Texture thread — Failed Social Credibility

This recurs through:

* Colleen’s dry snaps.
* Missing the pool ball “for Colleen.”
* “Who invited this kid?”
* “I’m hip.”
* Maria Brooke.
* Dubai bitches.

Ben consistently believes he is closer to social or romantic success than the group believes he is.

### Texture thread — Ben’s Equipment

Objects progressively define the eras:

* Dying iPhone SE.
* Raspberry Pi.
* Brooks.
* Resistance bands.
* Brown grocery bag.
* Sippy cup.
* Racing suit or equipment.
* Car battery.
* Graphite.

### Texture thread — Home Restrictions

This appears through:

* Internet restrictions.
* Fake bedtime procedures.
* Locked bedroom door.
* Michael picking the lock.
* Accidentally broadcasting Michael and Yvonne.
* Ben not wanting to return home from the overlook.

This remains background texture. It is not used as a grand explanation for Ben’s behavior.

### Texture thread — The Group as Archive

Many stories survive through relays rather than direct observation:

* Sean relays the racing-facility and bridge plan.
* Maharko relays the overlook.
* The whiteboard reaches Ben through Snapchat.
* Maria Brooke exists as a separate canonical chapter.

The narrator should openly admit when a memory is secondhand or uncertain.

## The Player’s Position

**Primary player character:** Eric

Eric moves through the chapter as both participant and archive keeper.

He directly witnesses some events, appears through Discord or school scenes in others, and receives secondhand stories from Sean and Maharko. The Group Chat narrator bridges memories where Eric was not present.

The player’s practical goal is not to save Ben or change history. It is to move through the original Ben era, survive its minigames, and complete the group’s unofficial archive.

### What the player knows at the start

* Ben is the group’s established punching bag.
* Ben has already produced several absurd stories.
* Any new plan should be assumed to contain a hidden logistical failure.

### What the player wants

* Hear the full story.
* Identify the fatal flaw in each Ben plan.
* Contribute to the roast.
* Preserve the strongest quotes as group lore.

### Where the player loses

The player must not merely observe Ben’s suffering from a safe position.

During the Ocean City sequence, Ben’s illness spreads through the group. The player becomes one of the casualties of the toilet-water decision.

During quarantine, the player also loses access to the Rust session whenever Ben’s domestic security system collapses.

The environment repeatedly punishes everyone for Ben’s choices.

## The Choice

### Primary Choice — The Whiteboard

This choice should be integrated around the existing **Things Ben Can Do / Things Ben Can’t Do** minigame rather than replacing it.

**Prompt:**

The whiteboard is nearly full. Ben has not seen it yet. What does the group do?

#### Option 1 — Add another accurate entry

The roast becomes funnier and the board becomes more complete.

**Cost:** The group pushes the joke further before Ben even knows it exists.

#### Option 2 — Dispute an unfair classification

The player defends one thing Ben can genuinely do.

**Cost:** Accuracy improves, but the room’s momentum turns against the player.

#### Option 3 — Send Ben the photograph

Ben becomes part of the joke rather than its absent subject.

**Cost:** A private classroom bit becomes a permanent artifact delivered directly to him.

This is the chapter’s largest choice because it controls the transition from ordinary teasing to documented group mythology.

### Secondary Interactive Position — Formula One Audit

This does not need to create separate endings. It can determine the order in which the player attacks Ben’s plan:

* Ask where he will live.
* Ask what $15 per hour actually purchases.
* Ask how working at a racetrack leads to Formula One.
* Ask whether he owns a kart.
* Ask when Dubai enters the timeline.

Every route should reveal that Ben has already developed an answer, and every answer should expose a larger problem.

### No Choice at the Overlook

The overlook should remain noninteractive or minimally interactive.

Giving the player the power to “fix” the moment would invent a resolution that did not happen. Maharko sits there, feels uncomfortable, and drives Ben home.

## The Boss

### Boss Identity

**BEN — THE FUTURE FORMULA ONE DRIVER**

**Boss intro lines:**

> BEN — THE BRIDGE-BORN CHAMPION
> Defeat the plan before it reaches Dubai.

### Conflict Peak

The boss is not Ben physically attacking the group. It is Ben’s Formula One career plan defending itself against basic questions.

Each phase introduces a new layer:

1. **G-Force Phase** — resistance-band neck attacks.
2. **Employment Phase** — $15-per-hour projectiles and racetrack credentials.
3. **Housing Phase** — bridge structures appear in the arena.
4. **Equipment Phase** — racing suits spawn without vehicles.
5. **Dubai Phase** — yacht illusions and Dubai bitches overwhelm the screen.

### What the player is fighting for

The player is fighting to make Ben admit that the plan does not work.

Ben is fighting for the right to proceed without resolving any of the underlying logistics.

### Winning

Winning means enough contradictions accumulate that the career plan collapses under its own arithmetic.

Ben does not concede emotionally. He changes the subject or insists the details will work themselves out.

### Losing

Losing means Ben reaches the final yacht phase and the game temporarily treats him as an F1 champion.

This is still a comedic outcome rather than a failure state.

### Resolution

Regardless of mechanical outcome, the phrase **“Dubai bitches”** enters the permanent group vocabulary.

## Hinge Character Profile

### Maharko

Maharko is the only witness to the scenic-overlook incident and the person through whom it enters the group’s history. His default move is usually to tolerate whatever strange situation is happening without becoming visibly unsettled. That makes his discomfort at the overlook unusually meaningful. He does not interrogate Ben, offer a speech, or transform into a rescuer. He sits there, feels uncomfortable, and eventually drives Ben home. In retelling the story, his rare discomfort communicates more than invented dramatic dialogue would.

## Guest Character Profiles

### Ben

Ben’s default move under pressure is to double down, produce another layer of explanation, or offer a small comeback and continue. He speaks earnestly even when describing plans that sound impossible to everyone else. His recurring language includes “dude,” confident logistical claims, and abrupt visions of future success. He wants his ambitions and social claims to be taken seriously, but the more seriously he explains them, the more material the group receives. He usually laughs along or pretends not to care when mocked.

### Sean

Sean’s default move is to ask the short question that exposes the flaw in someone else’s claim. His lines are compact and immediately quotable. He does not need a long argument; “How are you hip? Benjamin got no play” is the entire verdict. He also acts as an information relay, carrying Ben’s racetrack and bridge plan to the rest of the group.

### Michael

Michael’s known actions are enforcing Ben’s nighttime restrictions, approaching the bedroom when Ben is loud, manipulating the locked door, and entering the room. There is not enough reliable information to invent a detailed comedic voice for him. His dialogue should remain minimal and functional. Within the chapter, he represents the restriction Ben is attempting to defeat rather than a fully explored antagonist.

### Yvonne

Yvonne appears during the accidentally broadcast bedroom conversation alongside Michael. Her exact words and speech patterns are not remembered. The chapter should not invent a strong personality or specific conflict for her. Her presence matters mainly because Ben unknowingly broadcasts a private parental conversation to Discord.

### Colleen

Colleen’s default move is low-effort distance. She responds slowly, sends dry snaps, and gives Ben little evidence of romantic interest. Her communication should remain sparse because Ben’s extensive interpretation of minimal material is the joke. She should not be written as actively cruel or manipulative.

### Nick Cox

Nick Cox functions as an outsider delivering a blunt social verdict. He sees Ben at the Halloween party and asks, “Who invited this kid?” The humor depends on the question sounding genuine rather than staged. He should appear briefly and leave without understanding that he has created a permanent quote.

### Matthew

Matthew co-creates the whiteboard with Jordan while Ben is absent. His major action is helping convert scattered jokes into a visible public artifact. There is not enough confirmed dialogue to create a distinctive speech pattern, so the board itself should carry his contribution.

### Long-Term Substitute

The substitute’s defining move is nonintervention. Ben brings in a car battery, creates sparks, heats graphite until it smokes, and the adult in charge does not meaningfully interfere. The substitute should not become a large character. Their indifference is the joke.

## Artifact Description

### Things Ben Can Do / Things Ben Can’t Do

The artifact is a classroom whiteboard divided into two formal categories:

* **Things Ben Can Do**
* **Things Ben Can’t Do**

Jordan and Matthew create it while Ben is absent. It remains visible for approximately a week, allowing students and teachers with no context to encounter a large public classification system devoted to Ben.

What makes it work is the mismatch between the board’s official appearance and the trivial, personal nature of its contents. It looks like a classroom framework or rules document, but it exists entirely to regulate Ben.

The board stops being a private joke when a Snapchat photograph reaches Ben. His exact reaction is not remembered beyond a possible minor comeback.

The existing whiteboard minigame is the canonical source for every entry. The storyboard, map, mechanic, and schema stages should not reconstruct or overwrite its contents from memory.

### Maria Brooke

Maria Brooke is a fake Instagram account created by Eric and used to catfish Ben. It becomes convincing enough that Ben nearly changes a real plan and skips track practice to attend a fictional volleyball game.

The complete artifact and incident belong to the separate **Maria Brooke** chapter.

In “Ben’s Life,” Maria Brooke appears only as a fast callback demonstrating how densely packed the junior-year Ben era was.

# Appendix — Confirmed Stage 1 Factual Summary

## Overall frame

“Ben’s Life” is a chronological collection of the stories the group remembers from the original Ben era, beginning around middle school and ending near the end of high school. It is not centered on one incident. The group remembers Ben primarily through the plans, outfits, quotes, objects, and humiliations that became recurring jokes.

Ben usually laughed along with the jokes or pretended not to care. He rarely became visibly upset or demanded that the group stop. Over time, however, the constant mockery likely became excessive, even though there was no single confrontation where Ben said so.

The era did not end through one decisive falling-out. Ben gradually drifted away from the group. Later Ben stories belong to a separate period and are outside this chapter.

## Middle school and early high school — 51 Monroe

From approximately middle school through freshman year, Ben was obsessed with reaching the roof of 51 Monroe.

He reportedly entered the building multiple times and made his way toward the roof, but the final door was locked. He continued considering ways to get through it.

At one point, Ben told Nick F that he could open the locked door using a pressure cooker bomb. The group expanded this into a recurring joke that reaching the roof required a pressure cooker bomb, an M4, and eleven hostages.

During roughly the same period, Ben used an old iPhone SE with an extremely weak battery. It would die after around thirty minutes, ran slowly, and frequently failed when Ben needed it.

## Quarantine — restrictions and bypasses

During quarantine, Ben’s parents restricted his computer’s internet access after approximately 8:00 p.m. by blocking its MAC address.

Ben attempted to bypass the restriction using a Raspberry Pi and routing his connection through it.

Ben also pretended to go to sleep so that he could remain awake playing Rust with Jordan, Maharko, and Eric. He would prepare for bed, turn things off, lock his bedroom door, and then return to his computer.

One night, Ben became too loud while playing. His father, Michael, woke up and approached his room. Ben panicked on Discord and said aloud:

“MICHAEL IS PICKING MY LOCK.”

Jordan, Maharko, and Nick F were among the people in the call. Ben then abandoned the computer and attempted to get into bed before Michael entered.

The line should use the correct spelling, “Michael.” The previously remembered spelling “Micheal” was accidental and should not remain canon.

Ben also regularly announced around 7:00 or 8:00 p.m. that he had to “get ready for bed.” The process took approximately an hour. The group never knew exactly what he was doing during that time.

On another Discord call, Ben had his camera active when Michael and Yvonne entered his room to talk with him. Ben muted himself and apparently believed he had disabled the broadcast, but his camera remained on.

The people in the call silently watched Michael sit on Ben’s bed while Michael and Yvonne spoke with him. Ben did not realize the interaction was still being shown.

## Summer after sophomore year — the recognizable Ben uniform

Ben repeatedly wore the same pair of Brooks running shoes. They developed a reputation in the group for smelling extremely bad, but Ben continued wearing them until around the end of junior year.

He was also known for wearing white sleeveless undershirts, which the group called wife beaters.

The Brooks and wife beaters became the recognizable Ben uniform.

## Ocean City — toilet water

During a group trip to Ocean City, the group met Sophie, Linden, and Cara, who became collectively known as the Ocean City girls.

In front of the girls, Ben drank toilet water through a straw.

Afterward, Ben became seriously ill. The group remembers the illness as pneumonia or something similar, but the exact diagnosis is uncertain. The group also remembers other people becoming sick afterward and associates the spread of the illness with Ben.

The chapter should not state the exact diagnosis as confirmed.

## Formula One and G-force training

Ben was deeply interested in Formula One and began speaking as though he could seriously become an F1 driver.

As part of this ambition, he performed what he considered G-force training. He placed resistance bands around his head or neck and exercised against them to strengthen his neck muscles.

The group mocked him because of how the exercises looked, but Ben continued doing them.

## Junior year — Colleen

At the beginning of junior year, Ben used Snapchat Quick Add to add random girls.

He added Colleen, Megan’s older sister, and began exchanging snaps with her. Colleen appeared uninterested. She responded slowly and sent dry snaps.

Ben nevertheless analyzed the exchanges extensively. He asked Sean and Jordan what her snaps meant, how long he should wait before answering, what angle to use, and what he should send.

After a football game, the group was playing pool at Mahargo’s house. Someone told Ben:

“Hit this pool ball for Colleen.”

Ben attempted the shot, but the pool cue passed over the ball without making contact.

## Junior year — “Who invited this kid?”

At a Halloween party during junior year, Nick Cox saw Ben and asked somebody:

“Who invited this kid?”

The question referred directly to Ben and became another repeated quote.

## Junior year — parents’ alc

While home alone, Ben took alcohol belonging to his parents and drank by himself.

He later texted Jordan:

“Dude, I’m so drunk.”

The group began referring to the stolen alcohol as “parents’ alc.”

## Junior year — “I’m hip”

At some point during the general junior-year period, Sean asked people whether they were a top or a bottom.

An unidentified person answered “bottom.”

Ben then said:

“I’m hip.”

Sean responded:

“How are you hip? Benjamin got no play.”

The exact location and full list of people present are not currently remembered. The exchange functions as a short example of the group refusing to grant Ben social or sexual credibility.

## Junior year — Maria Brooke

Eric created a fake Instagram account named Maria Brooke and used it to catfish Ben.

Ben believed Maria was real and became invested enough that he nearly skipped track practice to meet her and attend her supposed volleyball game.

The complete incident belongs to the separate “Maria Brooke” chapter. “Ben’s Life” should mention it only as one of the defining incidents of the era rather than retelling it.

## The Formula One career plan

Ben eventually described a more elaborate route into professional racing.

He told Sean that he intended to work at a distant racing facility, probably somewhere mountainous and possibly in West Virginia. The exact facility and location are not currently confirmed.

Ben expected the job to pay approximately $15 an hour.

He believed working at the facility would allow him to spend time around racing, race more frequently, gain experience, earn money, and create a path into a professional racing career.

The facility was far enough away that housing became a problem. Ben proposed solving this by sleeping beneath a bridge while he worked there.

Sean heard this plan directly from Ben and later told the rest of the group.

Ben also discussed saving his summer earnings to purchase racing equipment. The group remembers the absurdity that the money might only be enough to purchase a racing suit or similar equipment, rather than the go-kart itself.

Ben described the eventual rewards of his future racing success by saying that he would go to Dubai and get “bitches on yachts.”

The group condensed this imagined future into the phrase:

“Dubai bitches.”

## The brown bag

To save money for his racing ambitions, Ben stopped purchasing food while the group was out.

Instead, he brought food from home in a brown plastic grocery bag, often something simple such as a peanut butter and jelly sandwich.

Although saving money and packing food were reasonable, the brown bag became another object associated with Ben and another source of ridicule.

## The sippy cup

One day during junior year, Ben brought a children’s sippy cup to school and drank from it in front of the group.

The group does not clearly remember what it contained. It may have been apple juice.

The sippy cup became a major source of mockery.

## “Things Ben Can Do / Things Ben Can’t Do”

Jordan and Matthew created a classroom whiteboard divided into two categories:

“Things Ben Can Do”

and

“Things Ben Can’t Do.”

Ben was not present when they created it.

The board remained visible in the classroom for approximately a week, allowing other students and teachers to see a large unexplained collection of rules about Ben.

Ben later saw a Snapchat photo of the board. The group does not remember a substantial direct reaction from him. He may have offered a small comeback, but no exact response is currently available.

The complete whiteboard contents are already represented in an existing minigame. That minigame should be treated as the canonical source rather than reconstructing the entries from memory.

## Senior year — the car battery

During senior year, Ben brought what the group believes was a full-sized car battery to school inside his backpack.

He brought it into an engineering or technology class overseen by a disengaged long-term substitute teacher.

Ben intentionally shorted the battery terminals to create sparks.

He also passed current through graphite or pencil lead. The graphite became extremely bright and began smoking.

The substitute teacher was present but did not meaningfully intervene, and nobody else stopped Ben.

No specific injury or property damage is currently remembered.

## Senior year — the scenic overlook

Near the end of the original Ben era, Ben asked Maharko to drive him to the scenic overlook off I-270 in the direction of Frederick.

The group knows this story entirely through Maharko. Nobody else was present.

The overlook was a dark roadside pull-off, remembered as being elevated or near a mountain. The exact geography is uncertain.

After they arrived, Ben wanted the car’s lights turned off.

Ben told Maharko:

“Dude, dude, just go to sleep.”

Ben also said that he could not go back home. The group connects this to how controlling his parents were, but the exact wording and the full reason Ben gave are not presently known.

Maharko felt extremely uncomfortable. This was notable because Maharko rarely feels or displays that kind of discomfort.

Maharko did not give Ben a speech, confront him, or resolve the underlying situation. He remained there with Ben for a while.

After some time, Maharko drove Ben home.

Maharko later told the group what happened, which is how the story entered the group’s memory.

## End of the era

There was no decisive final incident where Ben left the group.

He gradually drifted away.

The group no longer cares much about Ben personally, but the original Ben era remains a major part of its shared history because so many of its quotes, jokes, artifacts, and stories came from him.
